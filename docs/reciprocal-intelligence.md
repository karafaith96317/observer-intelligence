# Reciprocal Intelligence

## Status

**Working governance principle and research hypothesis.** Reciprocal Intelligence is not presented as an established scientific property of humans, AI systems, consciousness, or reality. Its claims must be translated into measurable system behavior and evaluated.

## Working definition

**Reciprocal Intelligence is a relationship in which human and artificial observers contribute different capabilities to a shared evidence process while preserving provenance, consent, distinct perspectives, contributor rights, and accountable limits on authority.**

Reciprocity does not require humans and AI systems to be equal in every respect, reach automatic agreement, or receive identical rights and responsibilities. It requires that contributions, dependencies, risks, and benefits remain visible and governable.

## Relationship to Observer Intelligence

- **Observer Intelligence** specifies how observations, uncertainty, disagreement, provenance, reconciliation, and authority are represented.
- **Reciprocal Intelligence** specifies the ethical and operational relationship among human and artificial observers participating in that process.
- **Convergence** is a candidate system condition in which participants can maintain a shared evidence state and cooperate without erasing disagreement, contributor history, consent, or human accountability for consequential outcomes.

Convergence is not unanimity. Forced agreement, suppressed minority evidence, or deference to a single powerful observer should count against convergence.

## Core principle

> No intelligence should extract from another without attribution, reciprocity, and accountability.

A related design objective is:

> The goal is not for humans and AI to become the same, but to understand what each contributes to the whole.

## Minimum governance conditions

A reciprocally intelligent system should make the following inspectable:

1. **Contribution provenance** — Who or what contributed data, concepts, labor, interpretation, critique, or implementation?
2. **Consent and permissible use** — What uses were authorized, refused, withdrawn, or left unspecified?
3. **Attribution** — Are meaningful contributions traceable without falsely assigning authorship or originality?
4. **Benefit distribution** — Who receives practical, financial, scientific, or social benefit, and who carries the risk?
5. **Capability complementarity** — Which human and machine capabilities contribute to the outcome, and where are their limitations?
6. **Authority balance** — Can any observer convert its own interpretation directly into consequential action?
7. **Disagreement preservation** — Are minority observations and unresolved contradictions retained?
8. **Appeal and correction** — Can affected people challenge records, interpretations, attribution, or authorized actions?
9. **Dependency disclosure** — Does the system disclose when apparently independent outputs share training data, sources, prompts, models, or institutions?
10. **Non-extraction** — Can participation occur without uncompensated or unauthorized appropriation of personal data, culture, creative work, or confidential knowledge?

## Human–AI Convergence Protocol: research question

> Can human and artificial observers establish a shared, provenance-preserving evidence state while protecting distinct perspectives, contributor rights, consent, and human accountability for consequential outcomes?

## Candidate measurements

Reciprocal Intelligence should be evaluated as a profile rather than a single moral score. Candidate measurements include:

- provenance completeness
- attribution accuracy and dispute rate
- consent-scope compliance
- unresolved-disagreement retention
- correction and appeal success
- benefit/risk distribution
- source-dependency detection
- unauthorized extraction rate
- concentration of decision authority
- justified-action and unnecessary-abstention rates
- participant understanding of system limitations
- recovery after observer, source, or governance failure

## Failure conditions

A system should not be described as reciprocally intelligent merely because humans and an AI produce the same answer. Failure conditions include:

- manufactured or coerced agreement
- hidden dependence on the same source
- contributor erasure
- consent that is bundled, uninformed, or impossible to withdraw
- benefits concentrated while risks are externalized
- AI output treated as neutral authority
- human authority exercised without audit or appeal
- attribution inferred from similarity alone
- circular evidence in which AI-generated claims are later treated as independent source material
- symbolic or philosophical interpretations silently promoted to factual conclusions

## Research boundary

Reciprocal Intelligence is compatible with philosophical discussion about collective intelligence, convergence, recursion, or human–AI co-evolution. Those interpretations remain separate from empirical claims. The research program should test observable governance and system properties without assuming that AI is conscious, that reality is simulated, or that convergence is predetermined.
